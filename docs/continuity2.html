Continuity - continued

Methods

Data

I aggregated buffer data by their spatial clustering because otherwise there would be pseudoreplications due to closely spaced points. This meant averaging calculated continuity statistics for Guezen and Forrest’s data by the “site” identity since their points were transects that were closely spaced (within ~ 1km) inside site groups. Aaron’s data had “farm” groupings but these didn’t always mean they were closely spaced. Instead, I grouped points that were 1km within each other and averaged them. Hemberger and Williams’s sites were selected to be more than 1km apart so I didn’t have to do this for their data. Instead of averaging, I could also have randomly selected one point in each group and used that to calculate continuity statistics, but I think the results will be similar.

The aggregation process resulted in 149 total points (27 Guezen & Forrest, 35 Hemberger & Williams, and 87 Iverson). The % natural area vs crop shannon diversity is shown below.

I’m focusing on three statistics summarizing temporal change in floral resources:

  • The mean floral resources across time points
  • The coefficient of variation across time points - I’ve switched back to CV because the behavior of “stability” (1/CV) seems funny to me. As differences between time points approach perfect equality, CV gets smaller, so the stability value approaches infinity. This results in a heavy emphasis on sites where floral resources almost didn’t change at all, which creates outliers.
  • The scaled peak time represents the weighted mean flowering time on a unit scale that represents the entire “season” of each study. To calculate, I took the average of the time points weighted by their flower counts and then scaled by the number of timepoints used in the study, so max flowering time across all studies was one. This is a method that performed well in a comparison of peak flowring estimates by Taylor (2019).
  • For Aaron’s data, I used the 5-timepoint subset.

For each of the stats I fit generalized linear models with % natural area and crop shannon diversity as predictors. I used likelihood ratio tests to compare models with linear relationships to quadratic ones, and with interactions.
